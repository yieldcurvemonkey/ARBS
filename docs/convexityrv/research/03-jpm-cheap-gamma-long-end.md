## 1.2 The two sources of "cheap gamma" identified
**Framing question, verbatim:** *"If short-dated vols are still rich, what is the best way to own convexity in such an environment? For this we once again turn to the long end of the curve."*

**Source A — conditional exposure to 10s/20s/30s renormalization (flow-driven distortion).**
- The distortion comes from convexity hedging concentrated in *long* maturities — unlike banks and actively-managed mortgage portfolios. Primary agent: **active management of variable annuities** — "these investors are delivered net short in a rally, particularly when equities underperform, as their liabilities extend." ## 1.3 The RV framework as stated in this note (Exhibit 5 footnote — verbatim, the single most load-bearing text)
> *"**Implied volatility on a 20s/40s flattener comes from solving for the level of prevailing volatility that would make the flattener's expected convexity P/L precisely offset 1-year slide under parallel rate shocks. Expected payoff comes from using swaption-implied vol to solve for the flattener's expected convexity P/L.** For details see An option by any other name, J. Younger et al, 2/3/17."*

Two dual metrics, both 1-year horizon, both under **parallel** rate shocks:
1. **Curve-implied ("swap-implied") vol** = σ* such that E[convexity P/L | σ*] = 1-year slide. Solve for σ*, compare to swaption-implied vol. If σ* < swaption vol → curve is the cheap gamma.
2. **Expected payoff** = E[convexity P/L] computed under the swaption-implied terminal distribution, net of slide. If positive → curve is the cheap gamma. (Same signal, expressed in bp of yield instead of vol.)

**Exhibit 5** plots both since Jan-16: 20s/40s swap-implied vol vs **1Yx30Y** swaption-implied vol (LHS, abp, axis 40–90) and expected payoff (RHS, bp yield, axis −1.0 to +4.0). Both are 1-year-horizon measures. As of 8/22/19 close the gap was wide and the expected payoff sharply positive — the point of the note.

## 2.3 The actual analytical content — the kurtosis signal for gamma vs vega
This is the note's real contribution and is the closest thing it has to a "gamma maintenance" doctrine: **when to be short gamma / long vega, conditioned on the realized distribution.**

**Signal construction (Exhibit 6 footnote, verbatim):**
> *"Significance of excess kurtosis is the **negated log-base-10 of the p-value** from a test for non-normal kurtosis (in practice, almost always excess kurtosis). A value of **1.3** denotes the threshold at which kurtosis is inconsistent with normally distributed moves to **95% confidence**."*
Measured on **rolling 1-month daily moves of the 1Mx10Y swap yield**. Chart range 0–4, Oct-16→Jan-18.

**Empirical result (Exhibit 5), 2013–present:**
- Selling **3Mx10Y** daily-delta-hedged ATMF, **held 1 month**, performed **substantially better** when initiated **following** periods of statistically significant ex-ante excess kurtosis.
- Selling **1Mx10Y** daily-delta-hedged ATMF, **held to expiry**, performed **substantially worse** when executed on days when ex-ante excess kurtosis was significant.
- Therefore **1M/3Mx10Y vol surface steepeners** (equi-notional calendar spreads = short front gamma, long back vega) carry the edge.

**The P&L decomposition logic (this is the note's answer to "how does a gamma-hedged position make/lose money"):**
> *"in a falling volatility environment, **implieds drop faster than realized** as fat tailed periods recede, leading to relative outperformance of short positions that **monetize vega P/L** compared to sales of shorter-dated structures that are **more driven by gamma returns**."*
> *"in a **rising volatility environment following a period of low kurtosis, implied should outpace realized, leading to long vega gains and gamma losses (assuming active delta management)**."*

So the decomposition they use is the two-bucket one: **gamma P&L ≈ f(realized) and vega P&L ≈ f(Δimplied)**, with the sign of the *spread* between them driven by the kurtosis regime. Delta hedging is assumed active and daily; it is the mechanism that isolates those two buckets, and no further granularity (theta bleed, hedge slippage, transaction cost) is given.

**Robustness methodology (Exhibit 5 footnotes, verbatim):**
> *"Trade performance measured with a **non-parametric ratio built from the average of 1) Non-parametric Sharpe: the average of median versus inter-quartile range; 2) Sterling ratio: median returns versus median losses; and 3) drawdown ratio: returns versus 5th percentile** as expected returns versus downside risk."*
> *"To judge how robust this result was, we computed trade performance on a **randomly selected subset of 50% of days** from both the high- and low-kurtosis samples, **repeating the exercise 1000 times.** Bars show the average performance metric from these 1000 trials, whereas error bars denote the 5th and 9th [sic — 95th] percentile performance."*

**State of the signal at time of writing:** *"our kurtosis metric has fallen substantially (and indeed has rarely spent much time above the 'significant' threshold this year)"* → position for the low-kurtosis branch: **short gamma / long vega.**

## 3.1 The core formulas
**Note on fidelity:** the two display equations are **mangled by the PDF text extraction** (the raw file contains fragments such as `PVBP + ` / `∆t = (−∆ ∗ 0.5 (∆ )` and `( − ) /PVBP [ ]` / `( )= + − [ ]` / `+ 0.5 σ /PVBP [cnv xnvcvalue]`). The reconstructions below are what the surrounding prose unambiguously specifies, and they are **verified numerically against the note's own exhibits** (see §3.2).

**(i) Second-order price approximation.** *"For some instantaneous move ('shock') in yields, ΔY, we can approximate the change in a bond's price using dollar duration (PVBP) and convexity (C):"*

```
ΔP  ≈  −PVBP · ΔY  +  ½ · C · (ΔY)²
```

**(ii) Expected return decomposition.** *"An agnostic view on the future (e.g. one where risks towards rising and falling yields are roughly balanced and the curve, in expectation, is unchanged) for an instrument with maturity M over horizon T for an initial yield Y, cost of funding F and expected volatility σ would translate into an expected return:"*

```
E[R]  =  (Y − F)·T          ← carry
       + Slide               ← roll down an unchanged curve over T, expressed in bp of yield
       + ½ · σ² · C / PVBP   ← "value of convexity"
```

*"In other words, even if we make an assumption about an unchanged yield curve we incorporate expectations about volatility around this baseline scenario. The **'value of convexity' is a function of 1) an instrument's convexity (itself strongly correlated with the maturity of the instrument) and 2) expectations about future volatility** of the instrument's price or yield: **the higher the expected volatility the higher the value of convexity**."*

Two important conventions that fall out of the numerics (§3.2):
- **σ is the ANNUALIZED yield vol**, even when the expected-return horizon is 3M. `σ_ann = σ_daily × √252`.
- `C / PVBP` ≈ **C/D**, has units of **years**, and for long bonds runs ~28y (30Y) to ~39y (50Y). The value of convexity therefore scales as **maturity² × vol²**.

**(iii) Risk-adjusted carry (RAC), Exhibit 2.** RAC = (horizon expected return in bp) / (annualized bp vol).

## 3.3 The options-based framework for pricing curve convexity (the operative RV engine)

**Step 0 — why a duration-neutral long-end flattener is a straddle (verbatim, Exhibit 3):**
> *"At inception, by construction, we're exposed to an equal dollar-duration in both the long (50Y) and short (30Y) bond positions. Thus under a small parallel shift in the curve, our total price return should be roughly zero. But as that parallel shift in size grows, the two bonds behave differently. The longer leg has approximately 20 years of additional cashflows that contract in PV terms more dramatically in a sell-off. Thus, in a parallel sell-off the dollar-duration of the longer tenor contracts more rapidly than that of the shorter tenor, and its price falls at a slower rate, leaving the trade with a positive P/L. In a rally, the opposite occurs... **duration-neutral flatteners with fixed hedge ratios will have positive P/L if the curve shifts in parallel in either direction. The resulting P/L diagram looks quite a bit like a straddle.**"*

Corollary, verbatim: *"**Of course, there is no free lunch. In principle, one should have to pay for this positive convexity with negative carry/roll. This biases the 30s/50s yield curve flatter, or often inverted, whereby one pays the higher rate in a flattener with positive convexity.**"* — Confirmed empirically across USD, EUR, GBP, JPY (Exhibit 4: 10-year max/min, average and inter-quartile range of 30s/50s swaps by currency; axis +20 to −60bp; all four typically inverted).

**Step 1 — expected payoff under a swaption-implied terminal distribution (verbatim):**
> *"**Specifically, we extract an implied distribution from ATMF and OTM pricing for each expiry. This is then multiplied with the payoff profile of an aged flattener at fixed coupon—primarily to incorporate carry costs—to estimate an expected return. Since, as discussed above, these trades tend to be located in longer-dated swaps, we use 1Yx30Y swaptions for a 1-year horizon and assume parallel shifts in rates.**"*

Exhibit 5 footnotes pin the construction exactly:
- Payoff: *"Net P/L for a spot 30s/50s flattener under parallel shifts in rate, **with coupons equal to the 1-year forward rates**."*
- Swaption-implied distribution: *"**The terminal distribution of rate shifts implied by ATMF and OTM 1Yx30Y swaption pricing.**"*

**Step 2 — solving for curve-implied ("breakeven") vol (verbatim):**
> *"**Alternatively, we can solve for the implied volatility priced into the long end of the yield curve. This can be accomplished by making a specific assumption for the terminal distribution of rates—in our case a normal distribution, again with parallel shifts in the curve—and solving for zero expected payoff over a given horizon. In other words, we are estimating the level of normal daily volatility in rates that is sufficient to offset the carry costs on a given curve trade.**"*

Exhibit 5 footnote: *"A **normal** distribution of terminal rate shifts **centered on zero** with **standard deviation fit to produce a probability-weighted payoff of zero** assuming parallel shifts in rates."*

**Step 3 — the RV signal, both directions (verbatim):**
> *"Both measures can be interpreted as a relative value signal for curve convexity trades versus swaptions. **When the expected payoff on a flattener using an implied distribution extracted from swaption pricing is positive, the curve trade is the cheaper source of long gamma exposure. The same can also be said when the level of volatility priced into the curve is less than that implied by ATMF swaptions.**"*

**Step 4 — the monetization statement (verbatim):**

# 4. Citi — "Rates Vol Lab: Forward steepener and vol divergence" (12 Jun 2023)
**Authors:** Mike Chang (AC), Jason Williams (AC), Andrea Appeddu (AC). 35 pages. File: `...Rates_Vol_Lab_Forward_steepener_and_vol_divergence.pdf.md`

## 4.1 The steepener = short vol relationship (the core of your item 4)
**The mechanism, verbatim:**
> *"We have previously highlighted the **inverse relationship between long-dated forward curves and implied vol due to the net short/(long) convexity exposure that is embedded in long-dated forward curve steepeners/(flatteners)**."*

This is the exact mirror image of the JPM framework: JPM says *long-end flattener = long gamma, priced with negative carry*; Citi says *long-end steepener = short gamma, compensated with positive carry.* Same trade, opposite sign, opposite institution's preferred direction — worth holding both in mind.

**Exhibit 4 — the divergence signal itself:** *"Vol and long-dated forward curve have moved inversely as expected until the recent divergence where the curve bear flattened slightly despite the sharp collapse in vol."* Plots **2y30y vol** (LHS, 75–110 axis) against the **20y5y − 10y10y rate spread** (RHS, inverted, −50 to −100bp), Jun-22 → Jun-23.
> *"The inverse relationship has been broadly consistent over the past year **until very recently** with the long-dated forward curve bear flattening slightly **despite the sharp collapse in vol**... While the divergence is still relatively modest at the moment, **it is worth watching** given that a hawkish skip at the FOMC meeting with upward revision in the median Fed dots could cause vol to remain muted with knee-jerk bear flattening in the curve. Given that the hawkish skip is the likely outcome, we prefer to **wait until after the FOMC meeting** to consider possibly establishing a **long-dated steepener as a positive carry / short vol trade**."*

**The divergence signal in operational terms:** vol collapses (→ steepener's short-convexity cost falls) while the curve fails to steepen (→ the carry compensation is not re-priced). That gap = the entry. Citi's discipline is to **wait for the event risk to clear** before putting it on, not to trade the divergence immediately.

## 4.2 The breakeven-vol standardization method (the quantitative core)
> *"**Because long-dated forward steepeners have net negative convexity, we can back out implied yield breakevens from the carry and compare them to the recent realized yield moves. Having a breakeven/realized vol ratio allows us to compare the positive carry of various long-dated forward steepeners in a standardized fashion.**"*

Mechanically: take the steepener's **1y carry** → solve for the **daily yield move** at which the negative-convexity loss exactly consumes that carry (**"daily BE, bp"**) → divide by **1y realized vol** of the spread. **Ratio > 1 = carry compensates for delivered convexity cost. Ratio < 1 = it does not.** This is the direct analogue of JPM's "breakeven vol," expressed in daily-bp rather than annualized-abp terms.

# 5. Cross-note synthesis — the unified curve-as-gamma framework

**The single trade, three vocabularies:**

| | JPM (2018/2019) | Citi (2023) |
|---|---|---|
| Direction preferred | **Long-end flattener** = long gamma | **Long-end steepener** = short gamma |
| Cost/compensation | Pay for convexity with negative carry & slide → curve trades **inverted** | Get paid for negative convexity with **positive carry** |
| Standardized metric | **Curve-implied ("swap-implied") vol**, abp, from setting E[convexity P/L] = slide | **Daily breakeven bp**, from setting convexity loss = 1y carry |
| Benchmark | 1Yx30Y ATMF+OTM swaption-implied distribution | 1y realized vol of the spread |
| Signal | swap-implied vol < swaption vol → curve is cheap gamma | BE/realized > 1 → steepener carry is adequate |
| Best structures | Forward-starting: **20Y/40Yx10Y**, 15Y/35Yx15Y, 25Y/35Yx5Y, **15Yx5Y vs 35Yx5Y** | **10y10y/20y5y**, 10y10y/15y15y, 15y5y/20y5y |

**The invariant across all four notes:** *value of convexity ∝ maturity² × vol².* Doubling vol quadruples convexity value (JPM Exhibit 17), which is why (a) long-end flatteners are cheap when swaption vol is high and the curve has not re-priced, (b) EGB 30Y+ flip from unattractive to attractive on a vol doubling (Exhibit 13), and (c) the Citi divergence signal fires when vol falls without the curve steepening.

**The three fair-value regressions you can lift and re-estimate directly:**
1. `30s/50s par gilt = 0.6 × (1Y convexity value) + 5.3 × (50Y par gilt yield) − 31` — R² 56%, se 4bp. *(How much curve slope is convexity: ~60% pass-through.)*
2. `1Y convexity value(3H68) = 0.49·x² − 0.6·x + 1.05` where x = daily bp vol — R² 99%. *(Confirms ½σ²C/PVBP with annualized σ; implies C/D ≈ 39y for a 50Y gilt.)*
3. `10s/20s/30s USD swap fly ~ f(10s/30s curve, VA liability duration)` — 5Y regression, R² 98%, se 0.5bp. *(The convexity-hedging-flow distortion in the belly of the long end.)*

# 6. Gaps and caveats
- **"An option by any other name" (Younger et al., 2/3/17) is the primary source for the framework and is NOT in the file set.** Both JPM notes defer to it for details. If exact numerical implementation matters, that document is the one to obtain.
- **"Zen and the art of gamma maintenance" does not contain delta-hedging bands, rehedge thresholds, or a formal gamma-hedged P&L decomposition.** The only cadence specified anywhere is **daily** (JPM backtest) and the only execution-timing rule is **Citi's "hedge in the afternoon / 1pm London delivers lower realized vol."**
- **The Feb-2018 display equations are OCR-corrupted in the source markdown.** The reconstructions in §3.1 are supported by the surrounding prose and verified against Exhibits 2 and 20; the raw file cannot be quoted for them.
- **Exhibit 6 (Aug-2019) axis values, Exhibit 8 point labels, and Exhibit 16 breakeven percentages are partially degraded by extraction.** Structure names and rank orderings are recoverable; some numerical labels are not.
- **No updated backtest exists in the Aug-2019 note.** The 56% / 86% hit rates belong to Feb-2018.
- The Citi conditional-curve and conditional-fly screens are explicitly **backward-looking illustrations, not trade recommendations** (their own footnote).
