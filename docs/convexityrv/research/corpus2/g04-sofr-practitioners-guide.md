## 3. SOFR-the-index facts that matter for a CA backtest (Ch 1)

- SOFR = **volume-weighted median** (50th pct) of TGCR + GCF + FICC-DVP bilateral repo filtered for specials (transactions below the volume-weighted 25th percentile removed), rounded to nearest bp, published ~8 a.m. ET next day; revised ~2:30 p.m. same day only if ≥1bp change. Fed also publishes 1st/25th/75th/99th percentiles and volume.
- Fitting a normal distribution to the published percentiles: **individual repo transactions have averaged a ~4bp standard deviation around SOFR; >10bp is exceptional** (Figures 1.7/1.8). This is an irreducible basis between any tradable repo and the settlement index.
- **SOFR futures cannot be priced by pure no-arbitrage** the way Treasury or ED futures can: SOFR is a volume-weighted time-series median and the volume data are not public, so no market participant can replicate the settlement rate via transactions. "Anyone trying to trade an arbitrage involving SOFR futures necessarily runs the risk that the published SOFR values … will be different from the repo rate used in the arbitrage." (Ch 1.) This caps how tight the futures-vs-OIS basis can be arbitraged, especially in the front contract.
- **Structural break at the SRF** (standing repo facility, announced July 28, 2021, min rate at top of policy window): pre-SRF history contains spikes (SOFR > EFFR for weeks; e.g., **June 29, 2018: SOFR 212bp vs EFFR 191bp — a standing 21bp "arbitrage"** blocked by balance-sheet costs); post-SRF the constraint SOFR ≤ SRFR caps spikes. The authors repeatedly warn that any statistical analysis (vol, mean reversion, spread regressions) mixing pre- and post-Aug-2021 data straddles this break — "like a series of swap rates, to which a cap is added in the middle of the time series."
- LIBOR–repo structural model (from H&S 2013 p.149): L − R = f(default prob p, recovery c, BIS risk weight q, capital ratio d, cost of equity g, marginal funding b). With p=0, L=b, q=20%, d=8%: **a 5% rise in cost of equity vs LIBOR widens LIBOR-repo by 8bp**; doubling the capital ratio doubles the spread. Spread increases in: rate level, cost of equity, capital ratio/risk weighting. Post-crisis balance-sheet constraints break the clean model → statistical model of Ch 4 (§8 below).

## 4. Contract mechanics: SR3 vs SR1 (Ch 2) — settlement and reference periods

**SR3 (3M SOFR futures)** — Figures 2.2/2.3:
- Delivery months: nearest **39** March-quarterly months. **Reference quarter: 3rd Wed (inclusive) of the 3rd month preceding the delivery month → 3rd Wed (exclusive) of the delivery month.** Contract month = month the reference quarter *begins* (so "Sep 2022 SR3" settles in Dec 2022 — same underlying window as "Sep 2022 ED" but settling at the END of the window, not the beginning).
- Last trading day: exchange business day preceding 3rd Wed of delivery month; cash settlement the next business day (the day the Fed publishes SOFR for the last trading day). A revision to the *last* day's SOFR cannot be reflected in the settlement price; revisions to all other days are (CME May 2018, p.6).
- Settlement rate: **ISDA daily compounding, Act/360, over business days**, R from Π(1 + SOFR_i·d_i/360)−1 scaled by 360/D (equation images lost in conversion; variables: n business days in the reference quarter, SOFR_i in % with a 1/100 factor per CME convention, d_i calendar days the fixing applies — a Friday fixing has d_i = 3 — D total calendar days). **Rounded to nearest 0.01bp**; price = 100 − rate. Note the d_i-weighting makes this "theoretically different from a strict geometric average" (Ch 5), though close enough for practical purposes at low rates.
- **Contract size USD 25 per bp per annum. Minimum price increment: 0.25bp for contracts with 4 months or less until last trading day; 0.5bp otherwise.** Product codes SR3 (CME), SFR Comdty (Bloomberg). Block minimums 250/500/1,000 contracts (Asian/European/Regular hours).
- Because more of the settlement rate becomes known through the reference quarter, front-contract volatility decays toward zero (Figure 2.5) — the futures "stickiness" the book links to Asian-option behavior in Ch 5.

**SR1 (1M SOFR futures)** — Figures 2.10/2.11:
- Delivery months: nearest **13 calendar months**; reference period = the calendar (delivery = contract) month; last trading day = last business day of the month.
- Settlement rate: **simple arithmetic average of SOFR over CALENDAR days** of the month (weekend/holiday carries preceding business day's fixing), **rounded to nearest 0.1bp** (coarser than SR3's 0.01bp).
- **Contract size USD 41.67 per bp per annum. Minimum increment 0.25bp during the delivery month, 0.5bp before.** Codes SR1 / SER Comdty. Blocks 125/250/500.
- Convention mirrors FF futures, so **SR1:FF is the clean listed expression of the secured–unsecured basis** (same window, same averaging); but the arithmetic average diverges from every compounded SOFR instrument and makes SR1 options arithmetic Asian (Ch 5).

**SR1–SR3 spread economics (Table 2.1 + Excel "3M versus 1M"):** CME lists the 1M–3M ICS as three SR1 each of the two mid-quarter months vs ten SR3 (BPV: 6×41.67 = 250.02 vs 10×25 = 250; residual <1bp). Fair value of the Sep-2022 spread (CME convention, bp) by (SOFR at start of ref quarter, slope over quarter): (0,0)→0.00; (0,25)→1.33; (0,50)→2.67; (100,−50)→−2.59; (100,−25)→−1.24; (100,0)→0.12; (100,25)→1.49; (100,50)→2.85; (500,−50)→0.15; (500,−25)→1.62; (500,0)→3.11; (500,25)→4.59; (500,50)→6.09. Compounding-vs-simple alone "reaches 3bp for a constant SOFR at 5% during the reference quarter." A hypothetical 50bp hike on Dec 1, 2022 (SOFR 10→60bp, outside the SR1 windows) puts the spread fair value at **12bp** — jumps in the uncovered tails of the reference quarter dominate.

**Compound-vs-simple magnitude on a 1Y term (Table 3.2, loan 3-Jan-22→3-Jan-23):** constant SOFR 0→compound 0; 100→101; 200→202; 300→305; 400→408; 500→**513** bp. (Known-answer tie-out for compounding code.)

**Rolls (Figure 2.9):** roll SR3 **before** the reference quarter starts (i.e., between the 2nd and 3rd listed contracts, not front/back as with ED). Dec-19 SR3 example: rolling into Mar-20 at the END of its reference quarter cost **138bp**; at the beginning, **4bp**.

**FOMC sensitivity formulas (known-answer tie-outs, Ch 2):**
- SR1: a hike on day k moves the contract by jump × (days in month after meeting)/(total days). **25bp hike announced May 4, 2022 → May SR1 moves 25 × (27/31) = 21.8bp.**
- SR3: recompute compounded rate with post-meeting fixings bumped. **Mar 2022 SR3 reacts 11.3bp to that same 25bp May-4 hike.** In USD per contract: sensitivity −25.02 to a 1bp level shift, **−11.27 to a 1bp jump on May 4**; May SR1 jump sensitivity −36.29.
- Jump-immunized hedge of 10 Mar-22 SR3: **short 3.1 May-22 SR1 + 2.9 Apr-22 SR1** (vs the naive 3/3). Off-center example (Sep-2018 SR3, meetings Sep 26 + Nov 8, 2018): **2.24 Oct SR1 + 3.79 Nov SR1 per 10 SR3** — total 6.03 1M contracts, i.e., >6 needed; explains why 1M futures sometimes "appear to move less" than 3M or the term rate.
- Strip with a partial front quarter: split the front SR3 into known/unknown fixings and solve for R_unknown (Excel "strip"); worked 6M strip starting Oct 3, 2022 with known SOFR 95bp and prices 99/98.5/98 → **annualized 6M strip rate 1.34%**.

## 5. The convexity adjustment (Ch 6 — the book's core contribution for this project)

Ch 6 carefully separates the two effects that the ED literature blurs under "convexity bias":

**(a) The financing bias (Cox–Ingersoll–Ross 1981).** Pure margining effect: if the futures *price* is negatively correlated with the short rate paid on margin (true for any rates future quoted 100−rate), shorts systematically reinvest gains at high rates and finance losses at low rates → the transacted futures price is pushed *lower* / futures rate *higher* than the forward. Zero if the margin rate is nonstochastic or uncorrelated. This exists **even for a linear contract** — "if two counterparties decide to subject an FRA to standard futures-style margining, the FRA rate will be subject to a financing bias as well."

**(b) The nonlinearity (Jensen) bias — ED yes, SR3 NO, SR1 slightly.**
- ED-style contract: payout at time S referencing term rate R_S(S→T) gets reinvested at R_S to T, making all-in time-T interest a **concave quadratic (parabola) in R_S**; by Jensen the futures rate must exceed the forward rate, with the required adjustment **increasing in time to expiry and in rate volatility** (Figure 6.1 compares FRA hedge — constant USD 5,000 at a 2% FRA rate on USD 1mm — with the futures hedge parabola).
- **SR3: "There is no nonlinearity involved."** The SR3 payout N·τ·(R₀−R_T) is received at T, the *end* of the reference period, and adding it to a balance compounded at the daily SOFRs gives exactly N·(1+τR₀) at T — "R₀ indeed plays the role of a forward rate for the three-month SOFR futures contract. There is no nonlinearity … that might require us to make an adjustment to the price of the contract before using it to construct a yield curve." Stated even more bluntly in the Introduction: "the three-month SOFR futures contract suffers from no such bias, **as the contract has no convexity**."
- **SR1: small positive bias from arithmetic averaging.** Daily compounding always ≥ arithmetic average (proof via first derivatives: compounding derivative carries the product of the other daily growth factors; holds for all non-negative fixings). Magnitude: "Given the low levels of SOFR prevailing recently, the difference tends to be no more than **a tenth of a basis point** — and often less"; "for simulated rate levels approaching 5%, the difference appears on the order of **1 basis point**." Advice: adjust when the difference is comparable to the 1M bid–ask; simulate when precision matters.

**(c) Is the financing bias priced? "Yes, probably" — but the authors are skeptical it should be paid for.** "If any futures contract is going to exhibit a correlation between the price … and the interest rate paid on margin, the SOFR futures contract is it. … In theory, the bias could amount to quite a few basis points, especially when the correlation … is high and when futures prices are volatile." Front contracts have the higher correlation with the overnight rate; back contracts give the effect longer to operate. Two counterweights:
1. **Everything is margined now** — FRAs, swaps, swaptions, caps: "if a futures contract and an otherwise similar FRA are both subject to futures-style margining, with interest paid on margin, it may not be critical to invest much time and effort making the adjustments, as the two products may well be adjusted by similar amounts." (Directly relevant to a cleared-swap-vs-futures CA: both legs margined, so the *differential* financing bias is what remains, driven by PAI/price-alignment vs futures margining differences — the book does not quantify this.)
2. **The Huggins prop-desk anecdote:** across one bank and two hedge funds, margin interest never appeared in desk P&L — even running 10,000 ED butterflies (40,000 contracts, USD 40bn notional) "we never included a financing bias in the analysis, because … we weren't paying or receiving any interest on margin." Conclusion: "the size of the financing bias in any complex, including the SOFR complex, is probably **best viewed as an empirical matter rather than as a theoretical matter**. And if you believe market pricing incorporates a material financing bias, and you don't have margin interest incorporated in your P&L, then you have an opportunity to get paid for subjecting yourself to a bias that doesn't affect you."

**Implication for the project:** for SR3, measured CA = financing bias + secured-curve microstructure + (for the front contract) settlement-replication risk — there is **no Jensen term** to model with Ho-Lee/Hull-White closed forms of the ED type. The book recommends computing financing-bias adjustments "in accordance with the term structure model they're using to price other derivatives — an approach we recommend for the sake of consistency if nothing else," but supplies **no formula and no magnitude estimate for SR3** beyond "quite a few basis points in theory." No Ho-Lee/HJM treatment anywhere; the model menu is Ch 2's (§6).

## 6. Term-structure models offered (Ch 2, Ch 5) — Vasicek/OU + FOMC jumps

- Framework: jump-diffusion with drift; pure jump (CME Term Rate) and pure diffusion as special cases. Preferred consistent model for futures AND options: "a one-factor **Hull-White model with jumps at the FOMC meeting dates** … when a jump occurs, both SOFR and the mean around which it reverts jump by similar amounts." (Suggested, not implemented.) Curve alternatives: step function jumping at contract dates; step function jumping at FOMC dates (+ year-end); splines (cubic > exponential at the short end) with FOMC dates as external variables (H&S 2013 ch. 11); spline+jump hybrid.
- **OU calibration of SOFR (proxy: overnight GC primary dealer survey rate back to 1998, weekly intervals):** estimated **mean 1.18%, speed of mean reversion 0.006** including spikes; **excluding spikes, speed 0.002** (Figure 2.18). Speeds are per-week on weekly data. The spikes-in-or-out choice alone moves the simulated Mar-2022 SR3 settlement from **99.4392** (speed 0.006) to **99.7615** (speed 0.002) — "even the question of whether to include a few spikes … can have a material impact on the model prices."

## 7. SOFR curve building (Ch 6) and the butterfly as a curvature penalty

- SOFR curves are **fitted, not bootstrapped**: the natural building blocks are FOMC-dated steps; more futures (13 SR1 + 5 SR3 in the Term Rate's case) than steps → overdetermined least-squares fit.
- **Table 6.1 — full input set, settlement prices 25-Feb-22.** 1M: Jan-22 99.95; Feb-22 99.8; Mar-22 99.635; Apr-22 99.37; May-22 99.225; Jun-22 99.095; Jul-22 98.935; Aug-22 98.875; Sep-22 98.735; Oct-22 98.585; Nov-22 98.475; Dec-22 98.395; Jan-23 98.3. 3M: Dec-21 99.9475; Mar-22 99.4975; Jun-22 99.02; Sep-22 98.635; Dec-22 98.345; Mar-23 98.095; Jun-23 97.95; Sep-23 97.91; Dec-23 97.92; Mar-24 97.995; Jun-24 98.06. (Note the Dec-23→Jun-24 price *rise* — inversion beyond 2023 already priced.)
- **Bootstrap tie-outs from that date:** Feb-22 step 0.0525%; step after 16-Mar FOMC 0.35733% (from Mar 1M) or 0.365% (from Apr 1M) or 0.36355% (LS on both); after 4-May 0.65574%; after 15-Jun 0.89426%; after 27-Jul 1.00005% (Aug contract; Jul skipped — too few days post-meeting); after 21-Sep 1.18833% (Oct contract; skipped Sep-22 1M then misprices by **nearly 7 cents**). Fitted 3M check: Mar-22 fitted 0.96 cents rich to settle, Jun-22 +1.75 cents. Average |error|: bootstrap 0.83 cents (first nine 1M) / 1.36 cents (Mar+Jun 3M), max 6.82 cents (Oct-22 1M); least-squares 1.10 / 0.50 cents, max 4.44 cents (Sep-22 1M), 0.65 cents (Mar-22 3M).
- **The butterfly enters as the smoothness penalty**: smoothness ≡ sum of squared butterfly spreads along the fitted step curve, fly = **(2×r2) − r1 − r3** on successive steps ("an approximation to the second derivative … relatively large in the presence of a reversal"). Objective = Σ squared price errors + λ·Σ squared flies. The raw fitted curve to end-2025 "oscillates beyond about May 2023"; the penalty suppresses exactly that oscillation (Figures 6.4/6.5). "The tradeoff between smoothness and quality is inevitable, and the choice is necessarily a subjective one."
- **CME Term SOFR uses the same device** (Ch 8): objective (8.2) = weighted squared futures-price errors + λ·Σ squared differences between *adjacent* steps. λ formula prints garbled in this conversion, but "the typical value of λ appears to be on the order of … roughly **0.33**," worth "slightly more than **half a basis point**" on the 6M term rate vs λ=0 on 27-Jan-22 data (Figure 8.1); very large λ moves the 6M rate "on the order of **30 basis points**." Authors suspect the penalty "probably imparts an **upward bias** to term SOFR rates" in upward-sloping curves (unproven, flagged by them as such). **Decision-relevant:** any published CME Term SOFR or λ-penalized fitted curve is biased *against* curve curvature — i.e., against exactly the fly-shaped local structure the CA-vs-fly project measures. A CA series built from a λ-smoothed futures curve will bleed fly signal into the CA residual by construction; fit with λ=0 (or report both) when constructing matched-maturity forwards from futures.
- CME Term SOFR mechanics (Ch 8/Fig 3.2): first 13 SR1 + first 5 SR3; day divided into 14×30-min intervals 7am–2pm CT; per-interval VWAP clipped to a randomly-timed bid/ask snapshot; daily input price = volume-weighted average of interval prices; **term rates depend on futures beyond the term's end** (global fit) — the 3M term rate can move on a contract whose reference period starts in 6M. Inputs are not published; the authors call the process "opaque by design" and ask for publication.

- Asset-swap taxonomy after SOFR: LIBOR asset swap ≡ LIBOR–repo basis swap ≡ (LIBOR–SOFR) + (SOFR–repo); for non-special bonds the second leg ≈ 0, so **SOFR-based asset swaps of Treasuries have theoretical fair value ≈ 0** (expected specialness only), modulo regulatory/balance-sheet distortions; the basis migrates *into* asset swaps of corporate/bank bonds. SR3:ED strips make the (previously theoretical) LIBOR–GC basis swap tradable.

## 9. Options on SOFR futures (Ch 5, Ch 10) — vol conventions and the Asian problem

**Specifications.** SR3 options (listed Jan 2020): American, USD 25/bp (USD 2,500/point), **last trading day = Friday preceding the 3rd Wednesday of the contract month — i.e., BEFORE the reference quarter begins** (ED options end the following Monday and cover the full term rate). Suite (Fig 5.1): 16 quarterly + 4 serial standard; 1Y–5Y quarterly mid-curves + serial mid-curves; 3M/6M/9M mid-curves; weekly mid-curves (nearest 2 Fridays) on 1Y/2Y/3Y mid-curves. Strikes: first 4 quarterly & serial — 6.25bp intervals over ±150bp around ATM, 25bp intervals over ±550bp; others 12.5bp/25bp. Ticks: 0.25bp if premium ≤5bp else 0.5bp (quarterlies). ED options "convert" to SR3 options by adding the ISDA spread adjustment to the strike. SR1 options (listed May 2020): American, USD 41.67/bp, expire **with** the future (last business day of the month), monthly standards nearest 4 months only; strikes 6.25bp over ±62.5bp, 12.5bp over ±125bp; tick 0.25bp = USD 10.4175. (OCR/typo caveat: the spec box prints "USD 4,147 per point per option contract"; 41.67×100 = 4,167 — treat 4,147 as a scan error.)
- Consequences: SR3 options never become Asian but leave the reference quarter **unhedgeable** with listed 3M options ("the problem of a missing pricing formula for the hedging instrument has been 'solved' by the hedging instrument itself being missing. It is similar to healing a wound by amputation"). SR1 options become **American-style arithmetic Asian options** during the month — "no pricing formula exists"; literature is European-only (Kemna–Vorst 1990 geometric control variate; Geman–Yor 1993 Laplace; Vyncke–Goovaerts–Dhaene 2003 moment-matched approximation usable for Greeks; **Fusai–Meucci 2008** discretely-monitored Lévy incl. jump-diffusion — the authors' recommended starting point, comparable to their Table 5.2).

**Process choice dominates option pricing (Table 5.2):** simulated average payoff of a call struck 100 on Mar-2022 SR3 (annualized bp; Vasicek start 0.05%, mean 1.5%, speed 0.002; only the May-4 meeting live): σ = 0/1/2/5% × P(+25bp hike) = 0/20/40/60/80/100%: 0% prob → 0.0/1.9/6.3/20.2; 20% → 0.0/1.8/6.1/19.2; 40% → 0.0/1.5/6.0/18.3; 60% → 0.0/1.3/4.8/17.6; 80% → 0.0/1.1/4.5/17.0; 100% → 0.0/1.0/4.3/16.6. A pure jump model prices the call at ~0; any diffusion prices it materially — hence the term-rate model (pure jump) and any option model are structurally inconsistent.

**Caps/floors (Ch 10, one line each):** hedging a SOFR floor uses SR3 options until they die, then SR1 options with a **known-fixings strike-adjustment formula** S_k = (n_t·S_t − n_k·A)/(n_t − n_k) (rearranged from the text's variables; e.g., floor 0.1%, two weeks of 0.2% fixings → roll the 99.90 call into the cheaper 100.00 call); ARRC's **daily floor** vs period floor commands a simulated premium of **44%** (single meeting, ±25bp equal probs, pure jump), **72%** (two meetings), **129%** (certain cut-then-hike), but is "limited" (~10% overhedge rule-of-thumb) under diffusion — another process-selection artifact (Table 10.1; several premium cells lost to table-conversion, the quoted numbers are from the text).

## 10. Hedging swaps and Treasuries with SR3 strips (Ch 9) — worked tie-outs

**The convergence claim (Figure 9.1):** post-transition, futures strips, SOFR OIS, and Treasuries are all "slightly different ways to compound the same reference rate … into a term rate"; single-curve pricing returns (floating leg PV = 100 at inception under matched conventions); the fair value of a Treasury SOFR asset swap ≈ 0 ⇒ each instrument replicates/hedges the others, and deviations are RV.

- **Empirics (Figure 9.2):** 1Y and 2Y CMT yield minus SOFR futures strip yield at the last four reference-quarter starts (Dec-20…Sep-21): small positive spreads, a few bp, larger at 2Y than 1Y; the one negative episode is **March 2020** (Treasuries exceptionally cheap to the strip during the crash). Semiannual-bond conversion applied: (1+S·(n·365+F)/360)^(1/2n) form (formula image partially lost; S = money-market strip rate).
- **IMM-perfect swap hedge (Excel "Swap hedge with strip"):** 1Y USD 100mm receive-fixed 1% IMM swap starting Jun 16, 2021 → short **102 Jun-21, 102 Sep-21, 101 Dec-21, 101 Mar-22** SR3 (>100 per quarter from Act/360; declining with the discount factor). Realistic version ("Swap hedge with strip 2": annual pay, Act/360 both legs, 2-day payment delay, hedged Jun 23 one week in): **101 of each** — near-identical at ~0 rates, "likely to change when the yield curve is not flat."
- **Treasury hedge (Excel "Bond hedge with strip"):** 1.5% UST note maturing Sep 30, 2021, hedged Jan 2, 2020, USD 100mm. Strip Dec-19…Sep-21; futures-implied PV 100.42 = **0.20 above market price** (≈11bp strip-vs-bond spread, consistent with Fig 9.2). Hedge (Table 9.1): **−101 Dec-19, −101 Mar-20, −100 Jun-20, −100 Sep-20, −100 Dec-20, −99 Mar-21, −99 Jun-21, −17 Sep-21 (total −717)** — the −17 reflects the bond maturing days into the last reference quarter; the last quarter has two FOMC dates (Sep 22 / Nov 3, 2021) but Dec-21-vs-Sep-21 futures only 1.5bp apart, so no jump fit needed. Parallel +1bp check: bond PV **−USD 17,568** vs hedge **+USD 17,517** (front-month P&L uses the reduced sensitivity factor 0.8384: (717−101)×25 + 101×0.8384×25). Rebalances at quarter turns (Mar 18, Jun 17, 2020): total contracts −717 → −632 → −531, per-contract counts drifting up (−100→−103) as rates fell and the curve flattened.
- **Hedge performance through Mar 2020 (Figure 9.4, unrebalanced):** Mar 2, 2020 — bond +1,162,500 vs futures −1,158,325 (near-perfect). **Mar 18, 2020 — bond +1,631,250 vs hedge −1,959,518 → net −USD 328,268, "about 20% of the overall move"** (SOFR asset-swap spread of the note went positive; the futures-swaps-bond triangle decoupled). Mar 31, 2020 — bond +2,100,000 vs futures −2,113,099, mismatch <1%; **the RV trade** (buy bond / sell strip on Mar 18 at the 328,268 dislocation) returned **+USD 315,169 in 13 days**. Authors' framing: the hedge works while the Figure-9.1 foundation (asset swap ≈ 0) holds; dislocations were "ephemeral" here and are the RV pond — this is the closest the book comes to the project's CA trade, in P&L space rather than bp.
- Corporate bonds: do NOT hedge with SOFR strips (reintroduces the secured–unsecured basis); use ED/FF.
- Front-month behavior is a *feature*: bond/swap and front future lose fixing-driven sensitivity at the same rate, so the hedge count is stable through the reference quarter and settlement roll-off is smooth (vs the ED strip cliff).

## 11. Simple hedges and CME Term SOFR hedging (Ch 7, Ch 8) — remaining tie-outs

**Ch 7 Example 1** (deposit 18-Dec-19→18-Mar-20, USD 50mm, d=91): desired BPV 0.01%×(91/360)×50mm = USD 1,263.89 → **50.56 contracts** (uses 50). **Ch 7 Example 2** (mismatched dates 2-Dec-19→28-Feb-20 vs the 18-Dec→18-Mar contract window): fit a 3-step FOMC curve to Dec/Jan/Feb SR1 settles (98.37 / 98.375 / 98.425) → steps **1.6300% / 1.6250% / 1.5750%** (Table 7.1, near-exact fit); out-of-sample check: fitted Dec-19 SR3 98.397 vs settle 98.395. +1bp bump: deposit +1,226.95; Dec-19 SR3 price +0.01004 → **48.88 contracts** (fixed at 50 ⇒ deposit sized 51,141,741). Outcome: deposit shortfall 6,742.88 vs futures gain **15,575** held to expiry (over-gain — the 12-day tail beyond the lending period is pure noise) or **9,375** if the hedge is lifted on Feb 28 — "sell the futures contract at the prevailing market price at the end of the lending period."

**Ch 7 Example 3** (same deposit hedged with 1M strip): per-period 1bp differences 403.27 / 432.26 / 391.42 USD ÷ 41.67 → Dec/Jan/Feb contract counts; realized shortfall 6,664.55 vs hedge return 6,379.68 → **net error USD 284.88 = 4.3%** — date-matched 1M strip beats the mismatched 3M contract. Lessons: hedge ratios are relative sensitivities to a chosen state variable; dates matter; parallel-shift assumptions are implicit and should be priced against their alternative's cost.
